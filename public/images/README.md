# Product imagery — what these are, and why

Every file under `public/images/` is an SVG illustration generated by
`scripts/generate-product-images.py`, written for this repository. Nothing was
downloaded, traced, or derived from a third-party image, so the whole set is
ours to publish with no attribution or licence obligation.

Naming: `<product-slug>/<colour-id>-<n>.svg`, where `n` is 1–3. The colour ids
match `OptionValue.id` in `src/server/demo-data.ts`, so the image on screen
always matches the variant the shopper has selected.

Each product *type* is drawn separately — an over-ear headphone, an open-back
grille, an in-ear bud, a crew tee, an oxford shirt with a placket and collar
stand, a camp-collar shirt with a patch pocket, a ribbed knit sweater, a chino
with belt loops and slant pockets, and a quarter-zip fleece with a grid face.
None of them is a recolour of another. Colour comes from the variant palette,
with pale colourways given a visible outline so a white shirt does not dissolve
into the near-white image background.

Regenerate after changing a product's colours:

```bash
python3 scripts/generate-product-images.py
```

## Why illustrations and not photographs

Photographs were the goal. They are not used because a set that would actually
work could not be assembled legitimately. What was checked, against Wikimedia
Commons:

- **Catalogue-style shots barely exist under a free licence.** Searching for
  plain-background product photography across all six product types returned
  essentially **one** genuinely usable free image — most "matches" were book
  scans, factory-floor documentation, a doll's jumper, and in one case a car in
  a field. The catalogue needs **78** images (26 colour variants × 3 views).
- **The clean shots that do exist are of real, identifiable products** — a Bose
  QuietComfort 25, an AudioQuest NightHawk, branded earbuds. Our catalogue is
  fictional, with invented specifications and written reviews. Putting a
  photograph of a real product behind an invented name and invented review text
  would misrepresent a real product, which is worse than an honest illustration.
- **No source offers per-colour coverage of the same garment.** The design
  requires the image to change with the selected variant. Photographing one
  shirt in three colours is a studio job, not a search result.
- Most free results are **CC BY-SA**, which places share-alike obligations on
  adaptations — workable, but it does not solve either problem above.

If you want photography, the two routes that would work are licensed stock from
a provider whose terms we accept, or shooting the items. Either way the images
would drop into `public/images/` under the same naming scheme and nothing in the
application would need to change. Until then, these illustrations are accurate
about what they are: drawings of products that do not exist.
